Who Indonesians are

At a glance

Indonesia is the world’s fourth most populous country and the largest economy in Southeast Asia. More than half of its people live on Java, an island about the size of England that holds roughly 55% of the population. The economy grew by about 5% a year through 2022–2024, inflation fell back to 1.6% in 2024, and the poverty rate dropped to 8.6% — the lowest on record. People

Population: 281.6 million people (2024)

Indonesia’s population grows by roughly 3 million people a year. Java remains the centre of gravity: the three Javanese provinces alone hold more people than the whole of the rest of the country’s islands combined.

    How the population is spread

    Java holds about 55% of Indonesians on about 7% of the land — Jakarta’s density is above 15,000 people per km², while Papua’s provinces are below 20. The capital’s move to Nusantara in East Kalimantan, announced in 2019 and under construction since 2022, is the government’s long-term answer to that imbalance.

    Median age is about 30 years, and the working-age share is still rising — a demographic dividend that peaks around 2030. Explore all 38 provinces →

    Money

    Economy: a $1.40 trillion economy (2024)

    Indonesia is the world’s 16th largest economy and the largest in Southeast Asia. Growth has held near 5% a year, driven by household consumption, nickel and commodity exports, and a fast-growing digital economy.

    Labour market

    What drives it

    Services now produce more than half of GDP, and manufacturing — food, textiles, electronics and vehicles — about a fifth. Indonesia is the world’s largest nickel producer and a top exporter of palm oil, coal and rubber. The rupiah trades at roughly Rp 16,000 to the US dollar (2024–2025), and Bank Indonesia’s policy rate ended 2024 at 6.00%.

    Wellbeing

    Health: living longer, still catching up

    Life expectancy reached 74.1 years in 2024, up from about 71 a decade earlier. The big public-health fight is stunting: one in five children under five was stunted in 2024, and the government’s target is 14% by 2029.

    How care is organised

    BPJS Kesehatan, the national health insurance scheme launched in 2014, now covers more than 270 million people — one of the largest single-payer systems in the world. Care is delivered through about 10,000 community health centres (puskesmas) plus hospitals, but doctors and specialists remain concentrated in Java’s cities: the national average is roughly 6 doctors per 10,000 people, against more than 20 in Jakarta.

    Learning

    Education: near-universal schooling, nine years on average

    Primary enrolment is effectively universal, and the average Indonesian adult has completed 9 years of schooling. The next frontier is quality: learning outcomes in reading and maths trail regional peers, and vocational training is expanding to match a young workforce to industry.

    How schooling is organised

    Schooling is compulsory for 12 years (6 primary, 3 junior secondary, 3 senior secondary), and about a fifth of the state budget goes to education by constitutional mandate. Indonesia has roughly 4,500 higher-education institutions, from state universities such as Universitas Indonesia and Gadjah Mada to thousands of private colleges, with about 10 million students enrolled.

    Why the regions differ so much

    Indonesia spans three time zones and more than 300 ethnic groups. Java’s provinces are dense, industrial and service-led; Sumatra and Kalimantan export palm oil, coal and gas; eastern provinces such as Papua and Maluku are sparsely populated, rich in minerals and forests, and furthest from markets. Provincial minimum wages in 2025 ranged from about Rp 2.0 million a month in the east to Rp 5.6 million in Jakarta.

    Nature

    Environment: forests, seas and a warming climate

    Indonesia holds about half of the world’s tropical peatland and the third-largest tropical rainforest, home to orangutans, tigers and rhinos found nowhere else. Protecting them while developing the economy is the country’s central environmental tension.

    Forests and climate

    Forest cover stood at about 95.6 million hectares in 2023 — roughly half the land. Deforestation fell sharply from its 2015 peak after a moratorium on new palm-oil and logging permits, though fires in dry El Niño years still burn large areas of peat. Indonesia has pledged net-zero emissions by 2060, with coal still generating about 60% of electricity and renewables at about 19% of the energy mix in 2024.
